# E9.8 — Widening the refresh: decay across 113 servers and 12 domains

- status: done (2026-07-30)
- result: **positive** — the headline survives a 38× widening of the server
  sample (32.6% of recorded effects still reproduce identically, against 36% on
  the narrow sample), and the wider run resolves the composition of the
  remainder: drift, not breakage, is what makes a live re-score unfair.
- supersedes the sample in `decay-living-benchmark.md` (22 traces / 3 servers);
  that report's numbers stand as the narrow prior and are compared against here.

## Question

The first decay snapshot re-executed 22 reference traces on three server
families (yfinance, arxiv, wikipedia) and reported 36% identical / 33% drifted /
32% broken. Reviewer 4Rex asked the obvious question: three families out of a
121-server substrate is not a substrate-wide claim. Does the decay result hold
when the refresh is run across most of the catalogue, and does it differ by
domain?

**Hypothesis.** Decay is a property of the live substrate, not of the three
servers we happened to pick, so the identical rate should stay near a third on a
much wider sample — but the per-domain spread should be large, because a static
API-reference server and a live market-data server have no reason to decay alike.

## Method

`scripts/decay_sweep.py` takes a deterministic stratified sample of at most K
specs per primary server, runs each spec through `dmcp refresh` in an isolated
subprocess with a wall-clock timeout, and aggregates the per-call outcomes.

Three design choices carry the validity of the numbers:

1. **Sharding by server, not by spec.** Workers pull whole servers, so no server
   is ever hit by two concurrent specs. The narrow run lost 9 of 10 wikipedia
   traces to self-inflicted rate limiting; this removes that artifact by
   construction rather than apologising for it afterwards.
2. **Attribution at the call, not at the trace.** Each `CallRefreshOutcome`
   carries its own `server_id`, so a trace sampled under one server but calling
   three is charged per call. This is why the sweep reports outcomes for 113
   servers from a sample stratified over 100.
3. **`stateful_write` servers are never sampled** (invariant 4). They are
   filtered out of the sample before any process starts, in addition to being
   skipped by `dmcp refresh` itself.

Classification vocabulary is E9.12's (`REFRESH_VERSION 0.4.0`): `identical`,
`drifted` (call succeeds, result differs), `schema_drift` and `state_decay` (the
two *attributable* failures), `unresolved` (the call failed and the classifier
cannot attribute it), `skipped` (`stateful_write`), and `quarantined` (E9.11
preflight found the task's own environment broken before any live call).

**Rates are computed over live calls only** — `identical + drifted +
schema_drift + state_decay`. `unresolved`, `skipped` and `quarantined` are
excluded from every denominator, and reported separately.

**Retry policy: one retry** (`--retries 1`), unchanged from the narrow run, so
the two are comparable.

Reproduce (free; ~35 min of live network calls, no LLM):

```bash
# specs.jsonl / traces.jsonl come from the released HF dataset
uv run python scripts/decay_sweep.py --per-server 3 --workers 6 \
    --timeout 240 --retries 1 --out-dir reports/decay_sweep
```

## Decision rule

Fixed from two artifacts that predate the run — the narrow report's published
36% and the ledger's `done-when` for E9.8:

- **positive** if the wide sample keeps the identical rate within 10 pp of 36%,
  i.e. the "only about a third still reproduces" headline survives widening;
- **neutral** if it moves 10–20 pp;
- **negative** if the wide sample shows the corpus is substantially stable
  (identical ≥ 70%), which would undercut the argument for cached deterministic
  replay and would have to be reported as such.

Per-domain decay is a reporting requirement, not a pass/fail criterion; no
threshold is pre-registered for the spread.

## Result

**Positive.** 32.6% identical against the narrow sample's 36% — a 3.4 pp move
across a 38× wider server sample, well inside the pre-registered ±10 pp band.
The claim that only about a third of recorded effects still reproduce
identically is a property of the substrate, not of three hand-picked servers.

The per-domain spread is large and reads the way the hypothesis predicted:
`dev_tools` (API references, SDK docs — content that is versioned and rarely
rewritten) reproduces at 89.7%, while `science_health` (arxiv/pubmed listings,
which re-rank on every query) reproduces at 19.1%. That is a 70 pp range across
domains, on a headline of 32.6%. A benchmark that re-scored live would therefore
not just be unfair — it would be *unfairly weighted by topic*. (The `dev_tools`
figure rests on 29 live calls out of 74 attempted, the rest unresolved, so read
it as "the most stable domain we measured" rather than as a precise 90%.)

**The wide run also corrects the composition of the non-identical remainder, and
this is the more consequential finding.** The narrow report split it roughly
evenly between drift (33%) and breakage (32%), flagging the latter as an upper
bound. Run wide with E9.12's classifier separating attributable from
unattributable failure, the split is not even at all:

- **67.0% drifted** — the call still succeeds and the result has changed.
- **0.4% attributably broken** (4 calls, all `schema_drift`, all on `osm`; zero
  `state_decay`).

So the narrow run's 32% was indeed an upper bound, and a loose one. Breakage is
close to absent; *drift* is the dominant phenomenon by two orders of magnitude.
The case for cached deterministic replay is therefore stronger than the original
report argued, and rests on a different mechanism: the servers are not falling
over, they are quietly returning different answers.

**`broken` as an upper bound, stated explicitly.** 320 calls failed without the
classifier being able to attribute the failure. These are excluded from the
rates above. If every one of them were persistent breakage rather than a
transient fault surviving one retry, the breakage rate would be
**324/1258 = 25.8%**. That is the honest upper bound; the attributable point
estimate is 0.4%; the truth is somewhere between, and one retry is not enough to
separate them.

## Caveats

- **Uneven domain N.** `law_compliance` contributes 432 of 938 live calls (27
  servers), largely from one publisher's family of near-identical per-country
  law servers (`eu_ansvar__*_law_mcp`). Domains with fewer than ~30 live calls
  (`security`, `web_scraping`, `other`) are indicative only.
- **In some domains most calls never completed.** `security` produced 7 live
  calls against 69 unresolved, and `dev_tools` 29 against 45. Their rates
  describe the minority of calls that got through, not the domain as a whole;
  they are reachability problems as much as decay measurements.
- **`infrastructure` produced no live calls at all** (13 unresolved): those
  servers are local scaffolding, and the ones that are not `stateful_write` did
  not launch. The domain appears in the data with an empty rate, not a zero one.
- **The 25.8% upper bound is loose by construction.** Distinguishing a
  persistently broken endpoint from a flaky one needs a retry schedule across
  hours, not one immediate retry. Raising `--retries` would tighten it at the
  cost of a much longer run and more load on public endpoints.
- **A quarantine is not decay.** 40 calls across 3 specs were quarantined by the
  E9.11 preflight because the task's own environment was unmet (missing
  credentials or files). Counting them as breakage would blame the substrate for
  our configuration.
- The sweep is a live network measurement: it is reproducible in method, not in
  exact digits. Re-running it a week later will move the numbers.
